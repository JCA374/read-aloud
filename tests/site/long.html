The Keeper of Skerry Light

When the old keeper finally retired, after forty-one years of climbing the same iron stairs twice a night, the harbour board sent a young engineer from the city who had never seen the sea in winter, never heard the foghorn answer itself across the bay, and never wondered why the gulls went quiet an hour before a storm.

She arrived on the supply boat in late October with two suitcases, a crate of books about optics, and a radio that only picked up the shipping forecast, which she listened to every evening at a quarter to six while the kettle boiled and the light above her began its slow, patient turning.

The first weeks were harder than she had expected, because the work was not the lamp itself, which mostly looked after itself, but the endless small repairs: the cracked pane in the lantern room, the generator that coughed whenever the wind came from the north, and the logbook, which the board insisted be kept in ink.

By December she had learned to read the sky the way the old keeper had, noticing the long grey swell that came before bad weather, the way the horizon sharpened just before rain, and the strange green light that sometimes hung over the water at dusk when the air was very still.

On the night of the great storm, when the waves broke over the gallery rail and the whole tower hummed like a struck bell, she stayed awake until dawn, counting the seconds between each flash, writing every hour in the logbook, and listening to the radio for a voice that never came.
